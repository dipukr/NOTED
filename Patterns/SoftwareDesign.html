Software Design

Software design is the tactical, low-level process of deciding how a piece of software will actually be built. This takes the high-level requirements and the architectural decisions and turns them into concrete classes, interfaces, methods, and the collaborations between them.

Where architecture answers the big questions — the what and why of major structural choices — design answers the how of a specific implementation, working within one component or service after its architecture is fixed.

Good design assigns each class a clear responsibility, keeps modules loosely coupled and highly cohesive, and hides detail behind well-chosen abstractions. It leans on established design patterns and principles such as SOLID, DRY, KISS, and YAGNI to keep code modular, reusable, testable, and easy to change.

The goal is not cleverness but maintainability: code that a future reader (often you) can understand, extend, and safely modify as requirements evolve.

Key points
  • Applies within a single component or service, after the architecture is set.
  • Chooses concrete design patterns (Strategy, Factory, Observer, ...) to solve local problems.
  • Defines class responsibilities, boundaries, and interactions.
  • Aims for high cohesion and low coupling so parts can change independently.
  • Guided by SOLID, DRY, KISS, YAGNI, and separation of concerns.
  • Favors programming to interfaces over concrete implementations.
Advantages
Modular and Testable

Small, focused units with clear boundaries are easy to reason about and test in isolation.

Localized Change

Well-placed abstractions keep an edit in one place from rippling across the whole system.

Reusability

Clean interfaces let components be reused in new contexts instead of copy-pasted.

Readability

Intention-revealing structure makes the code easier to understand and onboard new developers.

Adaptability

Loose coupling lets the system absorb new requirements with minimal rework.

Disadvantages
Over-Engineering

Too many abstractions add indirection and complexity that outweigh their benefit.

Premature Abstraction

Designing for imagined future needs can lock in the wrong structure and slow you down.

Analysis Paralysis

Chasing the perfect design can stall delivery and hide the real requirements.
